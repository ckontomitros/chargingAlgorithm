\chapter{Conclusions and Future Work} 
\label{ch:conclusions} 
In this chapter, the findings from the simulation project are summarized; the limits of the simulation are explained; and potential areas for further study as well as how to possibly improve upon them are discussed.

\section{Summary of Findings} 
\label{sec:summary} 
In this thesis, we have explored Smart Electric Vehicle Charging at Offices utilizing three forms of intelligence. The least sophisticated form is "Rule-Based" (Level ~1), then a generalized "Optimizing" (Level ~2) and lastly, an "Vehicle-To-Grid" (V2G)-enabling Optimizer (Level ~3). Our validation used a full year of 2018 data (365 days) and included actual PVGIS irradiance profiles for every single day. The subsequent sections will summarize the results of our analysis.

\subsection{Comparison of Intelligence Levels} 
\label{subsec:intelligence_comparison}

\subsubsection{Simple Rule-Based Charging} 
\label{subsubsec:simple_rule_based}

For Level ~1, we determined that the average daily costs for the entire year of 2018 were €29.79 with an overall yearly sum of approximately €10,873. Thus, Level ~1 was established as our baseline. During the complete twelve months of simulated operation no grid violations were detected. We determined that Level ~1 utilizes minimal computing resources while being relatively easy to predict.

\emph{Recommendations:} Rule-Based charging is recommended if you wish to maintain simplicity and are willing to accept limited processing capabilities. It appears applicable for small to medium sized installations (<5 -- >15 EVs) with simple chargers.

\subsubsection{Intelligent Optimization} 
\label{subsubsec:intelligent_optimization}

We also found that Level ~2 resulted in a reduction of approximately 1.1 percent of the annual average daily costs to €29.46. As a result, we calculated approximate annual savings of approximately €120. Also both levels utilized all available renewable energy. Similar to Level ~1 no constraint violations were identified during the 12 month period. However, since we implemented reinforcement learning (RL) algorithms (Q-Learning) in Level ~2 we needed to conduct a training phase. The training phase consumed significant amounts of resource.

\emph{Recommendations:} If you intend to implement your system in an area with a large number of vehicles (>10) we suggest using RL (particularly Q-Learning) as the principal methodology. Conversely, if your facility has fewer than ten vehicles, we recommend maintaining it as simple as possible with a rule-based controller.

\subsubsection{Vehicle-To-Grid Enabled Optimizing} 
\label{subsubsec:v2g_enabled_optimizing}

Level ~3 was tested under three different V2G pricing multipliers relative to wholesale buying prices: 80\%, 100\%, and 120\%. Under all three pricing multiples and for all 365 days simulated, V2G discharge occurred precisely zero times. Neither the basic algorithm nor the RL agent initiated any discharges. Regardless of the V2G selling price multiplier, the V2G enabled RL agent produced an annual average daily charge of €29.64 resulting in a yearly cost savings of €53 versus the simple baseline. The cost savings came solely from smart vehicle charging rather than revenue generated through V2G discharge.

The above findings represent a key understanding: at wholesale price ranges (€0.01 -- €0.06/kWh), the absolute difference in price is insufficient to support V2G arbitrage -- even when the sale price exceeds the purchase price by 20\%. The RL agent accurately learned that round-trip inefficiencies related to battery usage and the value of the loss due to reduced departure state-of-charge prevent discharging from being profitable.
\textit{Recommendation}: V2G arbitrage is not viable at wholesale electricity price levels regardless of the sell-to-buy ratio. For V2G to become profitable, at least one of the following conditions must be met: (i)~retail electricity tariffs (€0.15--0.30/kWh) providing a substantially larger absolute arbitrage spread; (ii)~greater peak-to-trough intraday price differentials; or (iii)~ancillary service payments (frequency regulation, capacity markets) supplementing the energy arbitrage value. \section{Research Questions Answered} \label{sec:research_questions} \subsection{What Type of Intelligence Yields Meaningful Gains?} \label{subsec:type_of_intelligence_yielding_meaningful_gains} \textit{Answer:} Level~1 (simple rule-based control) offers an elementary setup that is easily maintained. It operates within 1.1\% of what could be achieved with RL over a full year, does not require extensive infrastructure and computation, works reliably and predictably and can be implemented and operated relatively easily. Level~2 offers incremental gains (1.1\% over the annual simulation, €120/year savings) that may justify added complexity for larger organizations or when renewable utilization is an important objective.

\subsubsection{Battery Degradation Concerns}

Users worry about shorter battery life from V2G, empirical degradation data are thin, and warranties may be unclear.

\textit{Mitigation:} Offer degradation safeguards or insurance, cap discharge aggressively, and support decisions with measured data.

\subsection{Study Limitations}
\label{subsec:study_limitations}

The analysis is based on assumptions that include perfect solar production and building load forecasting; an idealized battery model which does not account for aging; fixed deterministic arrival times; homogeneity in the vehicle fleet; excludes individual driver preference information; and accounts for only one climate and building type (Paris, France). Since the simulation was conducted over a calendar year (365 days of 2018 weather data with real PVGIS irradiance) but each day’s run was an independent event and therefore did not capture higher-order dependence across multiple days (such as battery aging or weekly behavioral patterns) each of these limitations limits how directly the results will translate to actual field operations.
\section{Future Research Directions}
\label{sec:future_work}

\subsection{Short-Term Research Priorities}
\label{subsec:short_term}

\subsubsection{Uncertainty Quantification}

Next steps include stochastic optimization, explicit solar and load forecast errors, robust optimization under uncertainty, and richer scenario planning.

\textit{Expected impact:} More realistic performance estimates and policies that remain effective under uncertainty.

\subsubsection{Battery Degradation Model}
In the future, we will incorporate aging into our simulations (capacity loss, increased electrical resistance), assess how Vehicle to Grid affects battery life span, find ways to minimize degradation, and create an expanded economic model including costs associated with replacing batteries.

\textit{Potential impact:} Better defined business case for V2G; and degradation-aware control.

\subsection{Research Directions — Medium Term}
\label{subsec:medium_term}

\subsubsection{Analysis of Multi-Days and Seasons}

Even though this study models each day (1-365) in 2018 separately, it does not model how a battery degrades over time as a result of repeated charging and discharging. Future studies could be modeled with multiple days or weeks at a time to demonstrate how cumulative degradation affects the overall cost of an EV.
\subsubsection{Management of Heterogeneous Fleets}

There are still some open questions surrounding managing heterogeneous fleets of vehicles (different capacities, different recharge rates); conflicting objectives of users; fairness in allocating EV charging opportunities; and personalizing objectives among users.

\subsubsection{Advanced Forecasting}

Directions to consider include using machine learning based forecasting of solar radiation from weather information; occupancy-based modeling of building loads; forecasting of arrivals/departures of users; and tighter coupling of weather APIs and building sensors.

\subsection{Long-Term Research Vision}
\label{subsec:long_term}

\subsubsection{Advanced Artificial Intelligence Methods}

Deep reinforcement learning methods (e.g. A3C, PPO, SAC) are possible candidates for longer time horizons as well as multi-agent reinforcement learning; transferring knowledge across buildings; and federating learning for optimizing while preserving user privacy.

\subsection{Emerging Technologies}
\label{subsec:emerging_tech}

\subsection{Environmental Impact}
\label{subsec:environmental}

Supporting sustainability through increasing the utilization of renewable resources (and therefore reducing greenhouse gas emissions), enabling the grid to more easily absorb additional renewable sources via providing flexibility to the grid, and maximizing the utilization of current grid assets through flexible operation.

\subsection{Social Impact}
\label{subsec:social_impact}

Attracting employees through workplace charging; improving access to residents who do not have charging capabilities at their residences; adding distributed flexibility to enhance resilience; and demonstrating practical applications of artificial intelligence in energy management.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This study demonstrates that smart electric vehicle charging in commercial office buildings provides measurable economic benefits. Over a full year of simulation (365 days, 2018 data), reinforcement learning enables cost savings of 1.1\% (€120/year) compared to simple rule-based charging. The V2G sell price sensitivity analysis, conducted at 80\%, 100\%, and 120\% of the wholesale purchase price, reveals that V2G discharge arbitrage produces zero revenue at wholesale price levels. This is a structural finding: the absolute price spread at wholesale levels (€0.01--0.06/kWh) is insufficient to overcome roundtrip efficiency losses, regardless of the sell-to-buy ratio. V2G value creation therefore requires either retail-level tariffs, ancillary service markets, or significantly wider price differentials. The integration of solar power into the charging process is important for both the economic viability of the system and its ability to reduce greenhouse gas emissions. Furthermore, smart charging processes maximize the use of solar-generated electricity. Depending upon factors such as fleet size, tariff structures, local utility limitations, and organizational objectives, the optimal level of intelligence required may vary significantly.

\subsection{Closing Statement. }

As electric vehicle penetration increases and renewable resource capacity expands, smart charging will become increasingly important in the transition to a more sustainable energy system. Commercial offices offer an ideal setting for testing smart charging technologies because there is regular occupancy and often a source of renewable energy available (rooftop photovoltaic panels). This thesis demonstrates that coordination between EV charging and building load/PV generation can produce substantial economic benefits as well as environmental reductions.

Additional research, field trials, and policy support will likely be necessary before these concepts mature to the point where they can be implemented widely. However, technical feasibility and economic attractiveness for smart office charging have been established. The remaining question is when and how these systems will grow.
