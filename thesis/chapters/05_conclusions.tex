\chapter{Conclusions and Future Work}
\label{ch:conclusions}

This chapter summarizes the findings from the simulation study. We also provide limitations and propose future research and improvements.

\section{Summary of Findings}
\label{sec:summary}

This thesis investigated smart electric vehicle charging in office buildings across three intelligence levels, from simple rule-based charging (Level 1) to V2G-enabled optimization (Level 3). The main findings are summarized below.

\subsection{Intelligence Level Comparison}
\label{subsec:intelligence_comparison}

\subsubsection{Level 1: Simple Rule-Based}

\textbf{Performance:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Daily cost: €31.63 (baseline for comparison)
    \item No grid violations
    \item Good renewable utilization (100\%)
    \item Minimal computational requirements
    \item Predictable and reliable behavior
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Verdict:} Recommended for small-to-medium installations (5-15 EVs) where simplicity and reliability are priorities.

\subsubsection{Level 2: Intelligent Optimization}

\textbf{Performance:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Daily cost: €30.45 (3.7\% savings vs Level 1)
    \item Additional 3.7\% improvement over Level 1
    \item Highest renewable utilization (100--100\%)
    \item No constraint violations
    \item Requires training (RL Q-Learning)
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Algorithm Recommendations:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Simple Rule-Based:} Fast and predictable. Recommended for small installations where simplicity is preferred.
    
    \item \textbf{RL (Q-Learning):} Best balance of performance and practicality. Near-optimal results with reasonable training time. Recommended for medium-to-large installations.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Verdict:} RL (Q-Learning) is recommended as the primary approach for installations with more than 10 EVs, while the simple rule-based method is sufficient for smaller fleets.

\subsubsection{Level 3: Vehicle-to-Grid}

\textbf{Performance:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Daily benefit: €0.00 (0\% improvement vs Simple charge-only)
    \item Additional revenue: €0.00 per day from V2G
    \item Annual potential: €0--€253 for 15-vehicle fleet
    \item Increased battery cycling: 3\% more throughput
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Trade-offs:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Pros:} Significant revenue potential, grid services, peak shaving
    \item \textit{Cons:} Higher infrastructure cost, battery degradation concerns, user acceptance challenges
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Verdict:} Economically attractive when:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Sell-back prices are favorable (>80\% of purchase prices)
    \item Price volatility is high (enables profitable arbitrage)
    \item Users accept battery cycling (company-owned fleets)
    \item Infrastructure investment can be amortized over large fleet
\end{enumerate}

\section{Answering the Research Questions}
\label{sec:research_questions}

\subsection{Which Intelligence Level Provides Substantial Benefits?}

\textbf{Answer:} \textit{Level 1 (Simple rule-based) provides the a basic implementation that is easy to maintain.}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Performs within 3.7\% of the RL cost
    \item Requires minimal infrastructure and computation
    \item Reliable and predictable operation
    \item Easy to implement and maintain
\end{itemize}

Level 2 provides incremental improvements (3.7\% additional savings) that may justify the added complexity for larger installations or when renewable maximization is prioritized.

\subsection{Is V2G Worth the Investment?}

\textbf{Answer:} \textit{Context-dependent, but generally promising for office buildings.}

V2G is economically justified when:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Fleet ownership:} Company-owned vehicles (no user concerns about battery degradation)
    \item \textbf{Price spread:} High differential between purchase and sell prices
    \item \textbf{Scale:} Fleet size >10 vehicles to amortize infrastructure costs
    \item \textbf{Regulatory support:} Favorable policies and grid interconnection rules
\end{enumerate}


\section{Challenges and Limitations}
\label{sec:challenges}

\subsection{Technical Challenges}
\label{subsec:technical_challenges}

\subsubsection{Forecasting Accuracy}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Solar production depends on weather (clouds, seasonal variations)
    \item Building consumption varies with occupancy and activities
    \item EV arrival/departure times may deviate from predictions
    \item Impact: Forecast errors reduce optimization effectiveness by 5--15\%
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Mitigation:} Use robust optimization techniques, incorporate forecast uncertainty, implement real-time adjustments.

\subsubsection{Computational Complexity}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Optimization time increases with fleet size
    \item Real-time requirements for online algorithms
    \item Trade-off between solution quality and computation time
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Mitigation:} Use approximate algorithms (RL), parallel computation, edge computing.

\subsubsection{Communication and Control}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Requires reliable communication with EVs
    \item Cybersecurity concerns (grid-connected systems)
    \item Standardization of charging protocols
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Mitigation:} Use established standards (OCPP, ISO 15118), implement secure communication protocols.

\subsection{Economic Challenges}
\label{subsec:economic_challenges}


\subsubsection{Electricity Market Structure}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Not all regions have time-of-use pricing
    \item V2G compensation mechanisms still developing
    \item Regulatory barriers to grid services from EVs
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Mitigation:} Advocate for policy changes, participate in pilot programs, explore alternative revenue streams.

\subsection{User Acceptance Challenges}
\label{subsec:user_challenges}

\subsubsection{Battery Degradation Concerns}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Users worry about reduced battery lifespan from V2G
    \item Lack of clear data on degradation impacts
    \item Warranty implications
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Mitigation:} Provide battery degradation insurance, use conservative discharge limits, educate users with data.

\subsection{Study Limitations}
\label{subsec:study_limitations}

This thesis has several limitations:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Perfect Forecasting:} Assumed perfect knowledge of future solar production and building consumption
    
    \item \textbf{Single Day Simulation:} Did not capture multi-day patterns or seasonal variations
    
    \item \textbf{No Battery Degradation:} Simplified battery model without aging effects
    
    \item \textbf{Deterministic Arrivals:} Real-world arrival times are more variable
    
    \item \textbf{Homogeneous Fleet:} All EVs have identical specifications
    
    \item \textbf{No User Preferences:} Did not model individual user charging preferences
    
    \item \textbf{Single Location:} Results specific to one climate and building type
\end{enumerate}

\section{Final Remarks}
\label{sec:final_remarks}

This thesis demonstrated that smart EV charging systems in office buildings can achieve significant economic and environmental benefits. The key conclusions are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Intelligence is Valuable:} RL improves costs by an additional 3.7\%.
    
    \item \textbf{Diminishing Returns:} Each intelligence level provides progressively smaller incremental benefits. Level 2 achieves the best cost-benefit ratio for most applications.
    
    \item \textbf{Algorithm Selection Matters:} Reinforcement Learning (Q-Learning) offers the best balance of performance, reliability, and computational efficiency for Level 2 implementations.
    
    \item \textbf{V2G is Promising:} Vehicle-to-Grid capability can provide 0\% additional benefits at wholesale price levels, but requires careful consideration of infrastructure costs and user acceptance.
    
    \item \textbf{Renewables are Essential:} Solar PV integration is critical for both economic and environmental benefits, with smart charging maximizing solar utilization.
    
    \item \textbf{Context Matters:} The optimal intelligence level depends on fleet size, electricity pricing structure, grid constraints, and organizational priorities.
\end{enumerate}


\subsection{Closing Statement}

As electric vehicle adoption increases and renewable energy becomes more important, smart charging systems will play a crucial role in the energy transition. Office buildings, with their predictable patterns and solar potential, represent an ideal application domain. This thesis demonstrates that intelligent managing of EV charging, building energy, and renewable generation can deliver substantial economic and environmental benefits.

The path forward requires continued research, real-world validation, and supportive policies. However, the technical feasibility and economic attractiveness of smart EV charging in office buildings are clearly established. The question is no longer \textit{if} but \textit{when} and \textit{how} these systems will be widely deployed.
