\section{Summary of Results}
\label{sec:results_summary}

Results from simulations are divided into two scenarios: a multi-EV charge-only scenario with a fleet of fifteen vehicles, and a multi-EV V2G scenario which also includes that same fleet of fifteen vehicles without V2G functionality. Each scenario is further broken down into simple allocation schemes and RL allocation schemes.

Thus, V2G Arbitrage will require either higher absolute prices (i.e., Retail Tariffs) or much larger peak-to-trough differential pricing.

As can be seen from the table above, the results show no variation whatsoever when comparing the three sell back rates. As such, it is evident that neither V2G discharge energy nor revenue were generated by V2G discharge as a result of the three tested sell back prices. It has been established through the testing of these prices that the RL agent consistently demonstrates that at wholesale price levels (€0.01--0.06/kWh) a twenty percent increase in the sell back rate does not create sufficient margin to offset round trip battery loss and/or ensure that enough battery charge remains after departure to meet desired SoC targets. These constraints are fundamentally related to wholesale pricing rather than limitations in algorithms used for V2G arbitrage. In order for V2G arbitrage to be viable and profitable, at least one of the three conditions stated below must occur:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Absolute Prices Are Higher}: Retail electricity tariffs need to be significantly higher (€0.15--€0.30/kWh) to create a large-enough arbitrage spread.
\item \textbf{Increased Peak-to-Trough Differential}: A greater intraday price swing would yield increased profits from each individual V2G discharge cycle.
\item \textbf{Grid Service Payments}: Any ancillary services, including frequency regulation and capacity payments, may also help support revenue derived from energy arbitrage.
\end{enumerate}

In addition to understanding the effects of varying fleet sizes, there are additional algorithm-specific insights. These include understanding how a particular algorithm (i.e., reinforcement learning) impacts behavior in response to the environment.

We describe below some insights related to the learned policies produced by the reinforcement learning algorithms. We focus specifically on characteristics of the policies that emerge during training of the agents, including how they use information about future price levels to delay charging until later, when possible; how they favor charging during peak hours (when solar production is highest); and how they raise urgency as departure time nears. For the V2G case, we found that agents understand that selling power back to the grid is generally unprofitable and therefore avoid doing so unless absolutely necessary.
