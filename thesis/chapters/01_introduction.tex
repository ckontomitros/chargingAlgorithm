\section{Background and Motivation}
\label{sec:background}

Recent climate regulations have led to a rise in the adoption of electric vehicles. More and more people are opting for an electric vehicle for their daily commute. According to studies, most of the time spent in commute is on the way to and from the office. Also, most people spend a third of their time in offices, and with them their cars, 
which can be charged during the workday. The car batteries can also be used in times of peak consumption to give electricity back to the building or to the grid. Since the charging will happen throughout the day,  
it is logical to create smart charging algorithms that can utilise information from solar power generation, pricing spreads throughout the day, and grid capacity limits in order to optimise the charging schedule. Office buildings also offer a huge rooftop space for the installation of solar panels; 
the energy produced by these panels can be used both for the building's consumption as well as for the charging of the electric vehicles. The cost of electricity produced is zero, and this helps reduce the overall cost. As such, the utilization of renewable energy is the most important aspect of the charging algorithm. 

\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:literature_review}

There are many algorithms in play for the smart scheduling of the EV fleet. Early papers, such as Richardson \cite{richardson2012ev_ucd}, used linear programming to decide which vehicle will be charged when in 15-minute intervals, subject to network limitations. Later work included the use of reinforcement learning to improve grid stability, and optimise for pricing and renewables utilisation; several studies can be found in the work by Qiu et al. \cite{qiu2023rl_ev_review}. Below we discuss 10 representative papers from that review, all of which apply reinforcement learning to EV charging with the objective of cost minimisation and/or renewable energy integration, and compare to our present work.
\textbf{Wan et al. (2019)} \cite{wan2019drl_ev} proposed a model-free online drl-based real-time charging scheduling method for EV, using a representation network to extract features related to different time-varying electricity rates, as well as a Q-network that learns an action at every time step. They deal well with price uncertainty, but have a single EV and no renewable energy sources or building integration; we add a 15-EV fleet at an office building with rooftop PV and a stationary battery, and solve this problem using the same mechanism as the rest of this paper.
\textbf{Shin et al. (2019)} \cite{shin2019cooperative} presented a multi-agent deep reinforcement learning for cooperative management of photovoltaic/energy storage system-enabled charging station. They do consider PV and energy storage, but we are more focused on intra-building optimisation than interstation. We model building consumption, grid capacity as an optimisation constraint, and V2G capability in a single integrated scenario.
\textbf{Chiș et al.\ (2017)} \cite{chis2017rl_ev_price} developed one of the earliest RL approaches for plug-in EV charging with forecasted price signals, using Q-learning to reduce charging costs. Their study considers a single EV and focuses on price optimisation; we include utility from solar energy as a further optimisation objective and handle a multi-vehicle fleet under a shared grid capacity constraint.
\textbf{Qiu et al.\ (2020)} \cite{qiu2020drl_pricing_ev} propose a Prioritized DDPG method for the pricing of EVs with discrete charging levels, focusing primarily on the aggregator's perspective. Their work captures the V2G flexibility and pricing economics, but does not model on-site renewable generation or building energy consumption. Our thesis instead approaches the problem from a building operator's point of view, explicitly integrating renewable generation from rooftop PV production, and building loads into the reward signal.
\textbf{Dorokhova et al.\ (2021)} \cite{dorokhova2021drl_ev_pv} use deep reinforcement learning to control EV charging in a context with PV generation, comparing against rule-based and MPC benchmarks. Their formulation is the closest to ours in the list having PV integrated, but considers the problem of a single EV charging at a residential site. Our work differs in that we target a commercial office building with a fleet of 15 EVs, as well as a stationary battery, wholesale market electricity prices, and V2G capability.
\textbf{Tuchnitz et al.\ (2021)} \cite{tuchnitz2021smart_ev_fleet} suggest a smart charging strategy for an EV fleet using reinforcement learning to optimise fleet-level schedules and reduce the variance in the load sent to the grid by 65\%. Their study focuses on the load flattening and resulting stability of the grid, but does not treat the problem of renewable self-consumption or cost optimisation; our work jointly optimises for both solar utilisation and cost, whilst also enforcing constraints on the grid.
\textbf{Lee and Choi (2021)} \cite{lee2021dynamic_pricing_ev} introduce a privacy-preserving deep reinforcement learning framework for dynamic pricing and energy management of multiple distributed charging stations with PV and ESS. Their work addresses the pricing problem from the station operator's perspective using federated learning techniques. Our work differs in that we optimise from the point of view of the building operator, and use tabular Q-learning (which is understandable), rather than DDPG. We evaluate the profitability of V2G under real European wholesale prices.
\textbf{Abdullah et al.\ (2021)} \cite{abdullah2021rl_ev_review} offer an overview of RL-based EV charging management systems, categorising the algorithms used and comparing objectives. Their survey highlights that most studies opt for only one objective (cost, PV output) when in practice a complete algorithm that considers all is of much greater utility, although this kind of study is also hard to do under realistic assumptions. Our thesis helps fill in this space by performing a complete, integrated simulation on real European data, using a two-level approach and evaluating V2G viability.
\textbf{Sadeghianpourhamami et al. (2018)} \cite{sadeghianpourhamami2018scalable_ev} have introduced an scalable model-free demand response strategy using batch reinforcement learning (Fitted Q-Iteration) for coordinated charging of fleets. Due to its compact state representation, the scalability of Sadeghianpourhamami et al.'s (2018) approach is supported, however the focus of the reward function lies entirely on the load smoothing. In contrast to this, we have formulated the Q-learning to take into account a multi-objective reward with costs, solar usage, target SoC achievement and penalty terms for violations of constraints.

\textbf{Park and Moon (2022)} \cite{park2022madrl_ev} presented a multi-agent DRL strategy based on centralized training and decentralized implementation for scheduling EV charging. Their method allows multi-vehicle coordination via attention networks. However, they do not take into account on-site renewable generation nor V2G. We show here that even a much simpler tabular Q-learning method can achieve significant cost savings (of 3.7\%) while achieving the same level of target SoC as Park and Moon (2022), if the building PV generation and dynamic pricing data are incorporated appropriately into the Q-learning.

\section{Smart Charging Levels}
\label{sec:smart_charging_levels}

The algorithms developed in this thesis are separated by their intelligence levels. Level 0 is a simple rule-based algorithm that prioritises solar renewable energy, respecting grid constraints and the target charging SOC of the EV battery; it charges without respecting future price levels. 
Level 1 is a Q-learning algorithm that uses a reward function to include information about price levels and also the availability of solar energy. Level 2 is a Q-learning algorithm that also includes the action of discharging energy back to the grid. The remaining chapters focus on different parts of the algorithms.
