\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

\section{Background and Motivation}
\label{sec:background}

The global transition toward sustainable transportation has accelerated the adoption of electric vehicles (EVs). In 2023, global electric car sales neared 14 million and reached about 18\% of all cars sold, showing how quickly EVs are moving into the mass market \cite{iea_evo_2024}. As EV penetration grows, workplace charging becomes more important because it makes electric driving more convenient and affordable for employees and supports broader adoption \cite{doe_afdc_workplace}. At the same time, uncontrolled charging can stress the grid during peak hours, increase electricity costs, and leave on-site renewable energy underused. These problems are most visible in office buildings, where many vehicles connect at similar times and the electrical capacity is limited.

Smart charging systems address these issues by coordinating EV charging with building energy use, renewable generation, and time-varying electricity prices. Office buildings are a natural setting because parking times are long, usage patterns are regular, and rooftop solar is common. Solar PV is also increasingly cost-competitive, making local generation a practical part of workplace charging strategies \cite{irena_rpgc_2023}.

\section{Smart Charging Levels}
\label{sec:smart_charging_levels}

This thesis investigates multiple levels of charging intelligence. Each level adds coordination or control to the previous one, so we can compare benefits against added complexity. In this structure, the simple rule-based approach is the zero level.

\subsection{Level 0: Simple Rule-Based Charging}
\label{subsec:level0}

Level~0 uses basic rules to guide charging. The rules are simple, such as using solar surplus first, honoring grid capacity limits, and allocating power in a predictable order. This approach is easy to run and yields reasonable performance without heavy computation.

\subsection{Level 1: Intelligent Optimization}
\label{subsec:level1}

Level~1 uses optimization to compute better schedules. In this thesis, the main example is reinforcement learning, where a Q-learning agent improves charging decisions through trial and error. This level offers better performance but requires training and more complex implementation.

\subsection{Level 2: Vehicle-to-Grid (V2G)}
\label{subsec:level2}

Level~3 adds bidirectional energy flow so EVs can discharge back to the building or grid during high-price periods. This enables potential revenue and grid services, but it also adds infrastructure cost and constraints to protect battery health and mobility needs.

\section{Role of Renewable Energy}
\label{sec:renewable_energy}

Solar photovoltaic systems on office buildings can reduce charging costs and grid dependence. Solar production often peaks around midday, which aligns with typical workplace parking, so EVs can absorb on-site generation when it is available. This reduces grid purchases and helps lower peak demand, while also cutting emissions. Solar PV has become one of the lowest-cost new power options, which strengthens the case for integrating it with charging \cite{irena_rpgc_2023}. The main challenge is to coordinate charging with variable solar output while still meeting mobility needs and grid limits.

\section{Benefits of Smart Charging in Office Buildings}
\label{sec:benefits}

Office buildings are a strong fit for smart charging because arrival and departure times are predictable, parking durations are long enough to shift charging, and solar production typically overlaps with working hours. A single building operator can manage both the facility and the chargers, which simplifies control and makes cost savings more direct.

\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:literature_review}

\subsection{Smart EV Charging}
\label{subsec:lit_smart_charging}

Research on smart EV charging shows that coordinated scheduling can reduce costs and avoid grid stress compared with uncontrolled charging. Early studies such as Richardson et al. \cite{richardson2012electric} and Sun et al. \cite{sun2015optimal} formalized cost-minimizing schedules under grid constraints. Later work also explored decentralized and valley-filling strategies to manage peak demand while meeting user needs \cite{zhang2018smart}.

\subsection{Optimization Algorithms}
\label{subsec:lit_algorithms}

Optimization for charging ranges from classical methods to learning-based control. Reinforcement learning is attractive because it can handle uncertainty and learn policies directly from interaction. Sutton and Barto provide the foundational framework \cite{sutton2018reinforcement}, while applications to energy management and EV charging highlight its practical use \cite{vandael2013reinforcement}. Deep RL further expands the approach by handling larger state spaces \cite{mnih2015human}.

\subsection{Vehicle-to-Grid Technology}
\label{subsec:lit_v2g}

V2G technology enables bidirectional energy flow so EVs can provide grid services or earn revenue during high-price periods. Kempton and Tomi{\'c} established the basic revenue and capacity framework \cite{kempton2005vehicle}, and later studies examined control and aggregation strategies for services such as frequency regulation \cite{han2010development}. Reviews of V2G and related vehicle-to-x concepts highlight both the opportunity and the remaining technical and market barriers \cite{liu2019opportunities}.

\subsection{Renewable Integration}
\label{subsec:lit_renewable}

Renewable integration studies show that combining solar PV with smart charging can improve self-consumption and reduce grid draw. An NREL analysis of commercial EV charging highlights that co-deploying PV and storage with charging infrastructure can lower utility costs when charging is coordinated with on-site generation \cite{nrel_ev_pv_2019}.

\section{Research Objectives}
\label{sec:objectives}

The objectives are to develop models for intelligence levels 0--2, implement and compare a simple rule-based strategy and a Q-learning approach, quantify costs, renewable utilization, and constraint satisfaction, evaluate the added value of V2G, and provide practical guidance on which intelligence level offers the best cost--benefit balance for office buildings.

\label{sec:structure}

Chapter~3 describes the office building case study, data sources, and simulation setup. Chapter~4 reports the results and comparative analysis.
